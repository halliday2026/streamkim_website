## The Core Problem: When Does the Clock Start?

Few questions in California construction law generate more litigation than the statute of limitations. The limitations periods applicable to construction defect claims are not uniform — they depend on whether the defect is patent or latent, what type of construction is involved, whether the Right to Repair Act applies, and when the plaintiff knew or should have known about the defect. For contractors and developers, understanding this framework is essential to managing both defense strategy and litigation risk.

## Patent Defects: The Four-Year Period

A patent defect is one that is visible and discoverable upon reasonable inspection at the time of construction or delivery. California Code of Civil Procedure § 337.1 establishes a four-year statute of limitations for actions based on patent deficiencies in the planning, design, specifications, surveying, supervision, testing, or construction of an improvement to real property.

The four-year period under § 337.1 runs from the date of substantial completion of the improvement. "Substantial completion" is a term of art in California construction law — it does not require that every punch-list item be finished, but it requires that the work has reached the stage where it can be used for its intended purpose. Disputes about when substantial completion occurred are a common feature of construction defect litigation, particularly in cases where the project was completed in phases or where work continued after initial occupancy.

## Latent Defects: The Ten-Year Statute of Repose

A latent defect is one that is not visible or apparent upon reasonable inspection at the time of construction or delivery — a deficiency that is hidden within the structure or beneath a surface. California Code of Civil Procedure § 337.15 establishes a ten-year statute of repose for actions arising from latent deficiencies in the planning, design, specifications, surveying, supervision, testing, or construction of an improvement.

The ten-year period under § 337.15 is a statute of repose, not a statute of limitations. The distinction matters: a statute of repose runs from a fixed event (substantial completion) regardless of when the plaintiff discovered or should have discovered the defect. Once the ten-year period expires, the claim is barred — even if the plaintiff had no reasonable way to discover the defect before the period ran. This makes § 337.15 a stronger defense tool than the four-year patent defect period, and contractors and developers should be attentive to the ten-year mark on significant projects.

## The Discovery Rule and Its Limits

California's discovery rule provides that a statute of limitations does not begin to run until the plaintiff knew or, through the exercise of reasonable diligence, should have known of the facts giving rise to the claim. The discovery rule can extend the effective limitations period for claims that a plaintiff could not reasonably have discovered until after the ordinary limitations period would have run.

The discovery rule applies to claims under Code of Civil Procedure § 337.1 but is subject to important limitations. The rule does not extend a limitations period beyond the point at which the plaintiff had sufficient facts to bring a claim — awareness of a symptom of the defect (a leak, a crack, a settlement issue) may start the clock even if the plaintiff does not yet know the full scope of the underlying problem. Contractors and developers defending construction defect claims should carefully examine the timeline of when the plaintiff first observed or had reason to investigate the condition at issue.

Importantly, the discovery rule does not apply to the ten-year statute of repose under § 337.15. Because § 337.15 is a repose statute, the discovery rule cannot extend it beyond the ten-year period.

## The Right to Repair Act: A Different Framework for Residential Construction

California's Right to Repair Act (SB 800, Civil Code §§ 895–945.5) provides a separate framework for construction defect claims involving newly constructed residential units sold on or after January 1, 2003. The Act establishes specific functionality standards that residential construction must meet and requires a homeowner to provide the builder with notice and an opportunity to inspect and offer repairs before filing suit.

The Right to Repair Act has its own limitations periods, which differ from those under the Code of Civil Procedure. ## Practical Implications for Contractors and Developers

The construction defect limitations framework has several practical implications for California contractors and developers.

**Document substantial completion carefully.** The patent and latent defect limitations periods both run from substantial completion. A project completion log, owner sign-off, certificate of occupancy, or notice of completion recorded with the county recorder can help establish the substantial completion date with precision. Imprecise documentation creates ambiguity that plaintiffs can exploit.

**Investigate promptly when defects are reported.** When a property owner reports a construction issue, the investigation should be conducted promptly, even if the contractor believes the claim is without merit. Documentation of the condition at the time of the report — photographs, inspection reports, expert assessments — establishes a factual baseline that is valuable if litigation follows.

**Know the § 337.15 ten-year date for significant projects.** On large or complex projects, the ten-year repose date under § 337.15 is a milestone worth tracking. Once the date passes, latent defect claims arising from that project are barred. Some contractors and developers use the approaching ten-year date as an occasion to conduct their own inspection and assessment of the project's condition.

**Understand the Right to Repair Act's pre-litigation notice requirements.** For residential projects covered by the Right to Repair Act, the statute's pre-litigation notice and right-to-cure provisions are mandatory. Failure to comply with these provisions can affect the outcome of the subsequent litigation. Contractors and developers who receive a Right to Repair Act notice should engage counsel immediately to manage the response.
