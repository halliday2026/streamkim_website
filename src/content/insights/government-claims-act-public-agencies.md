## What the Government Claims Act Does

California's Government Claims Act (Government Code § 810 et seq.) governs the procedural pathway that any person must follow before bringing a civil action for money damages against a California state or local public entity. The Act's core requirement — the claims presentation requirement — has been a feature of California law for decades and reflects the Legislature's judgment that public agencies deserve advance notice of potential claims against them, with a meaningful opportunity to investigate and consider settlement before litigation begins.

For public agencies, the Act functions as a threshold defense: a claimant who fails to present a timely and proper claim before filing suit generally cannot maintain the action, regardless of the underlying merits. This makes the Government Claims Act one of the most practically important statutes in public entity defense.

## The Claims Presentation Requirement

Before filing a lawsuit against a California public entity for money damages, a claimant must present a written claim to the entity's governing body or designated claims agent. The claim must include specified information: the claimant's name and contact information, the date, place, and circumstances of the incident giving rise to the claim, a description of the claimant's injury or loss, the names of any public employees causing the injury (if known), and the amount claimed, if determinable.

The timing of the claim presentation depends on the nature of the claim. For claims involving personal injury, wrongful death, and property damage, Government Code § 911.2 requires the claim to be presented within six months of the date the cause of action accrued. For claims arising from causes of action not involving personal injury or property damage — certain contract and other claims — the period is one year. These deadlines are strictly enforced, and courts have limited authority to excuse untimely claims outside the procedures the Act itself provides.

## How Public Agencies Respond to Claims

Upon receiving a government tort claim, the public entity has 45 days to act on it (Government Code § 912.4). The entity may accept the claim, reject it in whole or in part, or return it as insufficiently stated. If the entity takes no action within the 45-day period, the claim is deemed rejected by operation of law. The entity's response to a claim — including what it says and what it does not say — can have significant litigation consequences and should be prepared with care.

Public agencies should establish claims-handling procedures that ensure timely receipt, routing, and response for every claim presented. A claim that is misrouted or lost, or one that receives a cursory response without adequate investigation, can create unnecessary complications in subsequent litigation.

## After Rejection: The Path to Litigation

Following rejection of a claim, the claimant generally has six months from the date of the written rejection notice to file a lawsuit (Government Code § 945.6). If the entity fails to give written notice of rejection, the period to file suit is extended — a circumstance that underscores the importance of proper written rejection of every claim.

A claimant who misses the six-month claims presentation deadline is not automatically barred from relief. Government Code § 911.4 allows a claimant to apply to the public entity for leave to present a late claim within one year of accrual, if the failure to timely present was through mistake, inadvertence, surprise, or excusable neglect. The entity may grant or deny the application. If denied, the claimant may petition the superior court under Government Code § 946.6. These late-claim procedures are a common litigation battleground.

## Practical Guidance for Public Agencies

Public agencies can improve their litigation posture under the Government Claims Act by attending to several practical considerations.

**Respond to every claim in writing.** Even when the claim appears to lack merit or to have been untimely filed, a written rejection starts the claimant's six-month litigation deadline running. Failing to respond leaves the timeline open and can give a claimant additional time to file suit.

**Investigate promptly.** The Government Claims Act is designed to give public agencies time to investigate before litigation begins. Using that window effectively — identifying witnesses, preserving evidence, and assessing liability — is one of the most valuable things an agency can do with the pre-litigation period.

**Consult counsel before responding.** The claims response is not merely a formality. What the agency says in its response — and what it declines to say — affects the shape of subsequent litigation. Public agencies should involve outside counsel in the claims-handling process for any matter with significant litigation potential.

**Distinguish the claims period from the limitations period.** The deadline to present a claim is separate from the general statute of limitations that applies to the underlying cause of action. Claimants who miss the claims presentation deadline cannot simply wait until the statute of limitations runs and then argue the claim is timely; the claims period is an independent procedural prerequisite.

The Government Claims Act is a powerful tool for public agency defendants, but its benefits are only available to agencies that understand its requirements and deploy them strategically.
