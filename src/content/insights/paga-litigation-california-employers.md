## What PAGA Is and Why It Matters

California's Private Attorneys General Act (Labor Code § 2698 et seq.), enacted in 2004, allows an aggrieved employee to file a civil action on behalf of the state to recover civil penalties for Labor Code violations committed against the employee and other current and former employees. Unlike a class action, PAGA does not require class certification — a significant procedural difference that has made PAGA claims attractive to plaintiffs' counsel and burdensome for employers.

The mechanics of PAGA differ from traditional employment litigation in ways that affect both exposure and strategy. A significant portion of penalties recovered in a PAGA action — 75 percent under the original statute — goes to the Labor and Workforce Development Agency (LWDA) rather than to the employees. The remaining 25 percent is distributed to the aggrieved employees. The civil penalty structure is capable of generating substantial aggregate exposure even in cases where individual employee damages are modest, which is why PAGA claims have been a significant source of employment litigation in California.

## The 2024 Reform Legislation

In June 2024, Governor Newsom signed legislation (AB 2288 and SB 92) that made the most significant changes to PAGA since its enactment. The reforms were the product of negotiations between business groups and labor advocates and reflected a bipartisan recognition that the prior PAGA framework had generated concerns about disproportionate penalties and the use of PAGA as a mechanism for litigation leverage rather than genuine worker protection.

## The Standing Question After Reform

One of the more significant aspects of the 2024 reforms involves the question of who has standing to bring a PAGA representative action. Under the prior framework, a single aggrieved employee could bring claims on behalf of all employees who experienced the same type of Labor Code violation — a broad standing rule that allowed individual plaintiffs to generate large aggregate claims. ## Practical Implications for Employers

The 2024 PAGA reforms create both new opportunities and new obligations for California employers. Several practical considerations deserve attention.

**Take advantage of cure and early resolution provisions.** The reforms expand the circumstances under which employers can reduce or eliminate PAGA exposure by correcting violations. Employers who have identified potential wage-and-hour issues should consult counsel promptly about whether corrective action is available and advisable.

**Conduct a wage-and-hour audit.** The best defense against PAGA liability is a compliant workplace. Wage-and-hour audits — examining pay practices, meal and rest period policies, timekeeping, and off-the-clock work — identify potential violations before they generate claims. An audit conducted in anticipation of litigation can also support privilege protections.

**Review your arbitration agreements.** The enforceability of arbitration agreements with PAGA waiver provisions has been the subject of significant litigation in California. The 2024 reforms do not resolve all questions in this area, and employers should ensure their arbitration agreements reflect current law.

**Respond quickly when a PAGA notice is received.** The PAGA process begins when an employee files a notice with the LWDA and the employer. Employers have a limited window to cure certain violations after receiving notice. The decision about whether and how to respond to a PAGA notice — including whether to make a cure offer — is a strategic one that should be made with counsel.

## What Has Not Changed

The 2024 reforms left the core PAGA mechanism intact. Employees may still bring representative actions for Labor Code violations on behalf of current and former employees. The LWDA still receives the majority of any penalties recovered. And the breadth of Labor Code provisions for which PAGA penalties can be sought remains substantial.

Employers who managed their PAGA exposure well before the reforms — through sound pay practices, current employment policies, and proactive compliance — are in a strong position under the revised framework. Those who have not addressed underlying wage-and-hour issues will find that the 2024 reforms, while beneficial, do not eliminate the exposure that follows from non-compliant practices.
